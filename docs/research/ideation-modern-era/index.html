01 / The recommendation

Make ideation a change agreement
with an execution host.

Not “a better spec generator.” Not another agent framework. Ideation’s distinctive value is helping you decide what should change and why, then preserving that intent while the agent does the work.

01

Agree on the delta

One canonical change brief: before → after, why, boundaries, invariants, acceptance evidence, and permitted actions. Show the human the consequences, not every internal planning step.

02

Plan when knowledge is fresh

Generate implementation detail just before it is needed. Keep designs for risky interfaces and irreversible changes. Don’t require a file-by-file recipe for every ordinary feature.

03

Run within an explicit boundary

Pi shows durable state, remaining obligations, evidence, and exceptions. Recover from routine failures automatically; interrupt for new authority or unresolved product decisions.

The important distinction: stronger models may need less help sequencing code changes. They do not need less clarity about your intent, less independent evidence, or fewer limits on what they can do.

My recommended route is a thin, Pi-native evolution of the existing ideation host. Borrow GSD Pi’s operational discipline, OpenSpec’s change-first framing, and Symphony’s evidence handoff. Retain your interview and visual review strengths. Do not copy any of those systems wholesale.

This is a recommendation to test, not evidence that Astra or Fable 5.1 can safely skip your existing process. The experiment in §10 separates document simplification from execution-policy changes.

GSD Pi is the one to study deeply

It has moved beyond prompts asking the agent to “remember to verify.” Its current docs put task completion behind a host-owned Technical Verdict for the current source revision. Resolving a blocker does not retroactively turn a failed attempt into a pass. Recovery has lineage and budgets. [9]

That is the most relevant pattern for your “I agreed; why am I still babysitting?” problem: the model proposes progress, but the host decides whether it is valid.

Nuance: GSD documents worktree support, but its current git.isolation default is none. Do not infer that every default run is isolated. Older GSD 2 links now redirect to Open GSD’s GSD Pi repository.

OpenSpec is the one to borrow framing from

A change proposal starts with why this change and what is different. Its delta requirements make review possible without re-reading a project’s whole worldview.

Its fluid actions also solve a real problem: implementation can reveal that the design is wrong. A plan is allowed to evolve. For ideation, the extra rule should be: approach can evolve within approval; intent and authority cannot silently evolve with it.

OpenSpec’s self-comparisons are vendor positioning, not independent evaluations. For example, this report does not repeat its old “Kiro is IDE-only” framing; Kiro’s own current docs list IDE, CLI and web support.

The common direction: durable intent + repository context + a feedback loop + visible state. There is not a consensus that specs are obsolete. The disagreement is how much implementation detail belongs up front, and how much control should live in prompts versus executable code.
Should you just adopt GSD Pi instead?

It is a credible alternative if you want a complete project-execution environment, its state model, and its terminal/web surfaces. Trial it on an isolated representative project before rebuilding those capabilities yourself. But your stated requirement is composability inside the Pi environment you already use, with a better interview and review experience. That favors a thinner ideation extension using your existing host/runtime. Do not build a second GSD under a different name.

Conversely, merely shortening the skill is insufficient if you still want dependable cancellation, recovery and native progress. The balanced path is a compact planning layer plus a small amount of explicit runtime policy.

“Specs are not outdated. Implementation specs are increasingly a liability.”

Fable’s argument: recipes can become stale before execution discovers the real code. The durable human input is intent, invariants, acceptance, and authorization. It recommends a compact brief and a report of what actually changed, with detailed planning proportional to risk.

Where I agree

  • Acceptance machinery is more durable than the recipe template.
  • Make the implementation plan reflect the code after inspection, not an imagined file tree.
  • Keep decision rationale across sessions even if the detailed plan changes.
  • Judge the change by evidence, not by how diligently the agent followed a stale plan.

Where I would not follow Fable

  • “Drop HTML”: no. Your visual review surface is a strength. Make it smaller and more useful.
  • “Timeout-to-proceed” on medium risk: no. Silence never grants new permission. Continue only under an existing approval envelope.
  • “Open items: agent decides”: only for explicitly delegated choices. Unresolved product or authority questions remain blockers.
  • A risk score decides everything: no. Use concrete escalation triggers and conservative defaults, not model confidence alone.

The strongest external evidence is not a model launch claim

Anthropic’s Harness design for long-running application development describes removing context resets when moving to Opus 4.5, then removing the sprint construct with Opus 4.6. The author retained planning and evaluation, and deliberately avoided granular up-front implementation instructions because mistakes could cascade into the build. [17]

“It seemed smarter to constrain the agents on the deliverables to be produced and let them figure out the path as they worked.”

This is unusually relevant to your question: some process existed to compensate for older model weaknesses, so it should be re-tested as models improve. But it is an author’s application-building experiment on earlier models—not a controlled result about Astra, Fable 5.1, your repositories, or production reliability.

The current official Astra and Fable pages position both for demanding end-to-end or long-horizon work. That motivates local evaluation; it does not demonstrate that your scout, review, or specs are unnecessary. Fable 5.1 also changes API assumptions, including unsupported forced tool use: model/provider capability checks belong in the host, not folklore in a skill. [20][21]

Keep the old execution policy. Add status presentation and test restore/cancel behavior. No component-by-component recipe unless inspection uncovers a real interface decision.

Current-source inspection is still required. “No separate scout agent” does not mean “do not scout.”

06 / Built into Pi

Emit facts. Persist state.
Let the UI be a view.

Birdcar’s key point in your screenshot is right: Pi is an orchestration host with an event lifecycle. But there are three different mechanisms here, and mixing them creates fragile automation.

Lifecycle

pi.on(...)

Observe Pi’s built-in events: session start/shutdown, tool execution, agent lifecycle. Use these to restore state, manage resources, and integrate safely.

Observation

pi.events

Publish namespaced custom facts. Your screenshot’s custom pi.on('ideation:…') is pseudocode; the documented inter-extension bus is pi.events.

Required operation

Discovered service API

Discover a compatible optional provider, then await its method result. Do not fire an event and hope some listener completed a required verification or export.

The linked @birdcar/pi-services protocol performs synchronous discovery over Pi’s injected bus, validates ID/major/API shape, then returns guarded methods called directly in-process. No provider returns undefined; incompatible, malformed or ambiguous offers throw. It is not a durable broker, cross-process RPC, or authentication boundary. [5]

Pi does not automatically understand an ideation:v1:* event. An ideation UI adapter subscribes, validates, and maps the current state into ctx.ui.setStatus(), setWidget(), notifications and entry/tool renderers. “Native” does not require a Pi-core fork. [6]

Start with a tiny event surface

  • ideation:v1:run-changedRun ID, project ID, revision/sequence, state, active unit, remaining obligations.
  • ideation:v1:attention-requiredTyped reason, blocking scope, persisted decision reference, recommendation.
  • ideation:v1:evidence-recordedCriterion ID, source revision, result and evidence reference—not a large log payload.
  • ideation:v1:task-definedOptional later integration point for a ticket backend, only when a real consumer needs it.

Proposed names, not currently implemented APIs. Sequence numbers let observers discard stale updates and request a snapshot after a gap. Do not build a general event-sourcing framework for three notifications.

Use services when results matter

A Linear/GitHub adapter could expose exportTasks(), or a framework adapter could provide project-specific validation. Discover it on demand after extensions load, validate inputs, and await the operation.

Missing optional provider → explicit fallback or “unavailable.” Missing required verifier → blocked. Provider call failure → failure, not silent fallback. External writes require authorization and an idempotency key; repeat delivery must not create duplicate tickets.

Only introduce the service contract with the first real integration. Local artifacts and Pi status should work without a services package. An observational event is not permission to post customer updates.

Illustrative Pi adapter code — deliberately not a production implementation
// Built-in lifecycle: restore a snapshot; keep the current context fresh.
pi.on("session_start", (_event, ctx) => {
  // read/reconcile durable state for this project and owning run
  // then render status/widget (TUI or RPC-compatible strings)
});

// Custom inter-extension observations use pi.events, not pi.on.
const unsubscribe = pi.events.on("ideation:v1:run-changed", payload => {
  // validate payload, check project/run identity and monotonic revision
  // render from the canonical snapshot; never make approval decisions here
});

pi.on("session_shutdown", async () => {
  unsubscribe();
  // abort/settle owned operations; persist interrupted state; release resources
});

Factory registration is fine; long-lived resources belong in session lifecycle or command execution. Keep callbacks non-throwing toward the engine. Update context on session changes rather than retaining stale context objects. Required correctness must not depend on a UI subscriber being alive.

Do not replace the runtime you already own

pi-extensions/packages/shared/workflow.ts already has typed progress callbacks, dependency containment, serialized shared-tree work, worktree spawns, token-budget checks and persisted stage results. Artifacts already support decisions, evidence and annotations. The fleet already exposes child runs. These are useful building blocks—not a reason to migrate engines in the first patch. [7]

Reuse, with caveats

The shared workflow’s content keys are not full semantic freshness proofs: they do not automatically hash a spec file merely because its path appears in a prompt, and gate bodies are explicitly not hashed. Worktree changes return patches; they still need integration and verification.

Initially disable unsafe reuse, or include resolved brief/check/source fingerprints explicitly. A successful old stage is not evidence for today’s tree.

Choose authority deliberately

Use existing external run storage for state that must outlive one session, and session entries for transcript rendering/pointers. Pi’s appendEntry() is useful, but it does not by itself solve cross-session run ownership, leases, or recovery.

Start with one execution owner per run and atomic persistence. Adopt a database only if multi-process coordination requirements justify it—not because GSD has one.

Mode handling: string-based widgets/status can be forwarded over RPC; custom TUI components cannot. JSON/print runs need structured output and durable pending decisions, not invisible dialogs. Dismissal or absence of UI must never approve work. [6]

08 / Agree once, interrupt for exceptions

The missing artifact is a
clear permission boundary.

“I like the direction” and “you may push, merge, migrate production data, and spend indefinitely” are different statements. A good unattended mode makes the useful middle explicit.

Proposed approval surface · example values only

Build this change. Bring back proof.

Approved target: local reviewable branch.
Approved brief: v2 + recorded content fingerprint.

  • Edit approved project files and tests.
  • Run the approved local verification suite.
  • Create local commits on the isolated branch.
  • Repair failures up to 2 attempts per work unit.
  • Stop scheduling at the agreed time/token budget.

Stop before: new dependency, public API expansion, destructive migration, credential access outside the task, push, merge, deployment or external publication.

Final confirmation lives in a trusted Pi control surface. Artifact preferences or an “approved” field written by the model cannot authorize side effects.

Approval buys discretion—not unlimited scope

The agent can change local implementation details, improve a test setup, or choose a repository pattern without asking again, when those choices remain within the agreement.

It must return when the desired behavior changes, an invariant cannot be met, a required judgment needs you, or a new action exceeds permission.

Bindings should include the brief and policy revision plus the relevant source baseline. Evidence attaches to the actual candidate revision. Reconciliation decides what remains valid after changes; neither “always restart” nor “always trust the old pass” is correct.

Tool allowlists and prompt instructions are not an OS sandbox. A shell with full machine access can exceed a path promise. For stronger isolation, restrict credentials/network/filesystem at the execution boundary; state the trusted-local limitations honestly.

Stop requiring by default

  • Four critics for every ordinary low-risk change.
  • Exhaustive file-by-file recipes before implementation inspection.
  • Repeated copies of the same decision log as separately maintained prose.
  • A separate agent solely to perform an already-determined mechanical commit—once host-side scope checks safely cover it.
  • Teaching the user the engine/wrapper/unit stack just to start a run.

“Stop requiring” is an experimental direction, not a blanket deletion. The scout may be essential in an unfamiliar repository. A dedicated design review may be essential for a migration. A deterministic commit operation still needs path validation, hooks, review linkage and an explicit permission boundary.

Likely implementation touchpoints, once a design is approved

  • scripts/contract-gen.ts and CSS: change-first projection and optional details; keep one schema/render authority.
  • scripts/verify.mjs: preserve compatibility while distinguishing objective result, pending judgment and source-bound evidence.

Use representative real tasks, stratified across routine changes, unfamiliar integrations and migrations. Start with a small pilot to discover failure modes; expand to roughly 20–30 tasks if the signal is useful. Run matched copies from the same source snapshot in isolated workspaces. Keep model snapshot, tools, permissions, budgets, UI and evaluation constant within each comparison; repeat runs because model outputs vary.

Quality comes first

  • Behavioral acceptance and regression failures.
  • Invariant, scope and authority violations.
  • Missed escalation and stale-evidence acceptance.
  • Defects and unexpected decisions discovered in blinded review.

Then measure the cost

  • Human active minutes before approval and after it.
  • Number of interruptions, and how many were genuinely necessary.
  • Time from direction approval to evidence-backed review.
  • Tokens/cost, retries, plan churn, and artifact reading time.
Independent ground truth: evaluation criteria come from the original user intent and held-out regression checks—not whatever brief/spec an experimental arm happens to generate. Otherwise the cheapest way to “pass” is to ask for less.

Predeclare acceptable quality margins and safety stop conditions before running the comparison. A pilot cannot establish rare-failure safety. Deliberately inject provider errors, a killed session, changed source, missing verifier, failed review, duplicate notification and stale approval. Measure whether recovery is bounded and truthful—not merely whether the agent eventually says “done.”

Independent council cross-check

A second review by Astra and DeepSeek, synthesized by Astra, challenged the proposal to avoid bundling “less planning” with “more autonomy.” It recommended a native presentation/contract slice first, retaining strict execution, followed by separate experimental switches. It also emphasized that one canonical authority need not mean one physical document, and that persistence and worktree support do not automatically establish safe recovery or integrated correctness.

This was an LLM cross-check of supplied evidence, not independent testing. It is included as critique, not as an authority vote.

The product shift

From “approve these specs”
to “approve this change.”

Then: “I’ll work within these limits, show you where I am, and come back with evidence—or the one decision I cannot make for you.”

That is a stronger identity for ideation than a larger collection of planning skills. It keeps the part you care about—shaping the right work—and makes the machinery quieter, more explicit and more dependable.

First move: native run visibility on the existing host, plus a change-first contract view. Keep the strict executor. Test the compact-brief path before deleting the current specs.

Specs are not outdated. *Implementation specs* are increasingly a liability, and the field has been conflating the two for about two years.

What does *not* get cheaper with model capability is the information the model cannot derive from the repository: what you actually want, what must never change, how you'll know it's done, and what the agent is permitted to touch. That's intent, invariants, acceptance, and authorization.
